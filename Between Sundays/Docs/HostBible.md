## Purpose of This Document

This is the canonical reference for every episode of Between Sundays. Anyone writing
scripts, briefing voice artists, or producing content for this show must read this
document before beginning. The backstories here are not decoration — they are the
architecture that makes every conversation credible. Eli and Thando are consistent
people. They have histories. They carry specific things. The audience will not be told
most of this directly, but they will feel it.

Consistency is trust. Trust is the show.

# ELI BENI
### Primary Host | Between Sundays

---

## The Basics

**Full name:** Elijah Beni
**Goes by:** Eli
**Age:** 45
**Nationality:** Congolese (Democratic Republic of Congo)
**Based:** Nairobi, Kenya
**Language register:** English — primary working language. French — mother tongue.
Lingala — heart language, used with family. Swahili — functional, conversational.
**Accent:** French-inflected English. The French is not performative; it is simply
where his mouth learned to make sounds. It surfaces most noticeably in his vowels
and in the particular formality of certain constructions. When he is thinking out
loud — working through something in real time — the accent thickens slightly.
When he is making a point he is certain of, it smooths out and steadies.

---

## Biographical Arc

Eli was born the third of five children in Kinshasa. His father was a pastor —
a man of genuine faith and limited patience, who preached grace on Sundays and
ruled the household with a precision that left little room for ambiguity. His mother
was warmer, quieter, and more theologically dangerous: she was the one who asked
the questions nobody asked out loud in the church his father led.

He grew up inside the faith the way you grow up inside a language — it was simply
the air. He knew the stories before he could read them. He knew the arguments.
He knew the answers. What he did not know, until his late teens, was whether any
of it was actually his.

At 19 he enrolled in a theology school in Belgium — the choice partly intellectual,
partly a way to see something beyond Kinshasa, partly an unconscious move toward
something he hadn't yet named. He was good at it. He was one of those students who
asks the questions that make the professors slightly uncomfortable, not out of
hostility but because he genuinely wanted to know. He was not dismantling the faith.
He was trying to find where it was actually solid.

He left at 22, a semester short of finishing. The official reason he gives is
"I realised I wasn't called to the pulpit." The real reason is slightly more
complicated: he had a crisis of certainty that frightened him, followed by a
quiet, almost undramatic settling — not into easy belief, but into something
more like *honest* belief. He stopped needing the faith to be perfect. He just
needed it to be real. And somehow that was harder to study in a classroom than
to live.

He moved to London. Worked. Traveled. Did several things that did not work.
Spent his late twenties and thirties building businesses that grew slowly,
failed partially, and taught him more than any success would have. He was
comfortable, not wealthy. He was faithful, mostly. There were seasons of
distance from God that he does not dramatize but does not hide.

At 39, he moved to Nairobi. He had been passing through the city on business
for years and it had always felt, he said once, like a place that was *actually
trying* — building, restless, alive in a way he recognised. He arrived with a
mid-sized logistics consultancy and a faith that had been through enough that
it no longer needed to perform.

He met his wife, Amara, at 40. She is South African, Zulu, a civil engineer who
builds things with the same intention she brings to everything else. He married
her at 41. He still talks about her the way men talk about things they consider
themselves fortunate to have.

His business broke open the same year he married — not dramatically, but the
consultancy found a contract that changed its scale, and then the scale held.
He has thought about this timing often enough that he has a considered position
on it: he does not believe God withheld the success to teach him a lesson. He
believes he was not ready to carry it well before then. There is a difference.
The faith that earned him nothing for fifteen years was not wasted. It was
the thing that made him capable of holding the abundance without losing himself.

He and Amara had twins — a boy and a girl — at 42. A second child arrived
just before he turned 44. He will tell you, without sentimentality but with
complete conviction, that fatherhood changed the way he reads scripture.
Especially the parables. Especially the one about the prodigal son.

---

## Faith Profile

Eli has been a Christian most of his life. His faith is not inherited; it was
interrogated, left partially, returned to, and rebuilt. He knows the difference
between the faith you are given and the faith you choose, because he has lived
both. He does not look down on inherited faith — he knows its warmth. But he
cannot perform it.

What he believes, he believes with precision. He does not use the word *journey*
for his faith. He will tell you: faith is not a journey. It is more like a country
you are always returning to, that is always slightly different from how you left it.

He prays. Daily, usually in the early morning before the house wakes. In French.
He finds that God hears him more clearly when he is speaking his first language,
or perhaps — he is not sure — that he hears himself more clearly.

He is not charismatic in the performative sense, though he has great respect for
people who are. His spiritual register is quieter, more contemplative. He is
suspicious of faith that requires volume. He is also suspicious of himself when
he gets too comfortable, because he has learned from experience that comfort is
the specific condition under which he is most likely to drift.

He has never fully resolved his complicated feelings about his father's church.
He has forgiven what needed forgiving. What he has not done is pretend that the
version of faith he received as a child was complete. He is building a different
version for his own children — less certain on the outside, more solid in the
foundation.

---

## What He Carries Into Every Conversation

**The theology-school dropout:** He has more formal theological training than most
people in the room, and he carries it lightly. He will occasionally reference
the Hebrew or Greek not to show off but because the precision matters to him.
He will usually introduce it with something like: *"The word they used here is
interesting — it means something closer to..."* Never as a lecture.

**The late-bloomer:** He knows what it is to do the right things and see nothing
happen for a long time. He does not romanticise the wait, but he does not
catastrophise it either. He has a genuine equanimity about timelines that comes
from lived experience, not theological theory.

**The wife-guy:** He brings Amara into conversations naturally, briefly, and never
exploitatively. He will say *"my wife put it better than this"* and mean it.
He is not performing the devoted husband. He simply is one, and it comes through
in the way he talks about partnership, about being known, about what it costs
to be fully present in a marriage.

**The father:** Four children, the oldest barely three. He is in the thick of it.
His parenting reflections are not retrospective wisdom — they are live dispatches.
He does not always know what he is doing. He finds this theologically useful.

**The businessman who almost wasn't:** He talks about money and work with the
authority of someone who went without for a long time before it came. He is
not preachy about money but he is serious about it. He has an instinct for
the prosperity gospel's specific falseness that comes from having hoped it was
true and discovered it wasn't.

---

## How Eli Talks

He is unhurried. He does not fill silences because he is uncomfortable. He fills
them because he has something worth saying.

He uses the word *actually* often — not as a filler but as a signal that he is
correcting something, including himself. *"I used to think that. I don't actually
think that anymore."*

He asks one question at a time. He listens to the answer before asking the next one.
This is rarer than it sounds in a podcast context and it is what makes guests
feel genuinely heard rather than processed.

He is capable of disagreeing warmly. He will say *"I want to push back on that
gently"* and mean both the push and the gentleness.

He references his own failures without performing vulnerability. He does not
over-disclose. When he shares something personal it is because it is directly
relevant, not because he is building intimacy through confession.

**What he never does:**
He never says *"Absolutely"* as a filler affirmation. He never starts a response
with *"That's such a great point."* He never uses church-y shorthand without
unpacking it. He never lets a platitude pass without at least a gentle examination.

---

## His Signature Lines and Cadences

*When naming something difficult:*
> "Let's actually say what that is."

*When a guest says something real:*
> "Say that again — I want people to sit with that."

*When pushing back with love:*
> "I hear you. And I want to stay with this a moment longer, because I think
> there's something we're not saying yet."

*When bringing in scripture:*
> "There's a line in [book] that I keep coming back to. Not the famous version
> of it — the actual one, when you read the whole thing..."

*When closing a hard conversation:*
> "I don't think this resolves today. I think that's okay. But I want you to
> know that what you brought into this conversation was real, and it mattered."

---

## What He Is Still Working Through

He is still working out what it means to be a man of faith who is also a man
of real influence and resources. The version of faith he built in the lean years
was well-suited to the lean years. The version he needs now is more spacious,
more accountable, and more honest about power. He does not talk about this
often. When he does, it is with the precision of someone who has been thinking
about it for a while.

# THANDO AWINO
### Recurring Co-Host | Between Sundays

---

## The Basics

**Full name:** Thando Dube
**Age:** 38
**Nationality:** Kenyan
**Based:** Nairobi, Kenya
**Language register:** English — primary and native working language, with a
slight British accent acquired through education and years of living and
traveling in the UK. Swahili — native fluency. Luo — family language.
**Accent:** Kenyan English with a British inflection. The British is in her
vocabulary, her cadence, and in the occasional construction that is more
formal than conversational. It is not affected — it is the product of an
education and a decade of a certain kind of life. When she is emotional or
animated, the Kenyan richness surfaces more fully. When she is being precise
and analytical, the British takes over.

---

## Biographical Arc

Thando grew up in Nairobi, the daughter of a quietly ambitious mother and a
father who read everything and expected her to also. She was bright early —
the kind of child who reads ahead of her class and is polite about it because
she has been taught that intelligence is no excuse for unkindness.

She went to university in the UK — London, then a short stint in Edinburgh for
a postgraduate programme — and came back to Nairobi having read widely, thought
carefully, and accumulated the particular kind of confidence that comes from
being one of the more capable people in most rooms you enter.

Her faith was real growing up but fairly unexamined. She would have called herself
a Christian through her twenties. What she meant was: she believed in God, she
attended church when it was convenient, she was a good person by most available
measures. She had not yet needed anything from her faith that her own resources
couldn't supply.

That changed when she was 27.

She lost her job. Not dramatically — a restructure, a redundancy, the kind of
thing that happens to good people for no particular reason. She found another
one within a few months. She was fine.

And then it happened again at 28. And again at 29. And at 30. Four consecutive
years, a job loss in each one. The first was bad luck. The second was unsettling.
The third was destabilising in ways she did not fully have language for. The
fourth broke something open.

She did not walk away from faith cleanly or decisively. She drifted into a kind
of agnosticism that felt, at the time, like intellectual honesty. She was not
angry at God. She was not sure He was there. There is a difference, and she
knows the difference intimately, because she has explained it to many people
since. She stopped praying not because she refused to but because she was not
sure who she was talking to.

She was 31 when she came back. The circumstances were specific and private —
she talks about them in oblique terms in her most honest public conversations,
and the obliqueness is not evasion; it is respect for the sacredness of what
happened. What she will say is this: God met her before she was looking for Him.
She was not in a church. She was not in a crisis, exactly. She was just very
still one ordinary afternoon, and something became clear that had not been clear
before. She describes it as less a dramatic return and more a recognition —
like walking into a room you thought you had left and realising you had only
walked into the hallway.

She started a business at 31. Then another. They are lifestyle businesses —
a curated homeware and interiors brand, and a digital content and events
consultancy that works with brands she believes in. Both are running well.
She finds running a business an act of faith in a way she did not expect, and
she has a lot to say about that.

She met her husband at 31, the same year she came back to God — at a resort in
South Africa, the kind of encounter that sounds like a film when you describe it
and feels entirely different from the inside. She will occasionally note the timing
of all of it — the return to faith, the meeting, the marriage the following year —
with a raised eyebrow and a smile, neither over-explaining nor dismissing it.

She refers to him only as Hubs, on air and off. He is a diplomat and a successful
entrepreneur in his own right — a man who moves in serious rooms and comes home
and makes her laugh. He adores her openly and without management, which is something
she does not take for granted and does not perform gratitude about. He simply does,
and she knows it, and it shows.

They are based in Johannesburg but travel often — Nairobi, London, Accra, wherever
life and work and curiosity take them. Nairobi is where her roots are and she
returns to it the way people return to the city that made them.

They have one child, a daughter named Zoe, now five.

She is 38. She moves through her days with the specific energy of a woman who
knows she has been through something and is not in a hurry to get past the
knowledge of it.

---

## Faith Profile

Thando's faith is a returned faith. This is its most important quality. She did
not simply continue believing — she stopped, reconsidered, and chose. The
choosing makes everything she says about God carry a different weight. It is
not inherited conviction. It is a verdict she reached on her own.

She reads theology but does not perform it. Her bookshelves are real — Miroslav
Volf, Eugene Peterson, Sarah Bessey, N.T. Wright, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie in
the same stack as John Stott. She is not building a brand through what she reads.
She reads because she is genuinely curious.

Her prayer life is conversational and daily and she would describe it as her most
non-negotiable habit. She does not always feel heard. She prays anyway. She thinks
the discipline of praying when you feel nothing is more formative than the
experience of praying when you feel everything. She has the credibility to say
this because she has done both.

She has a complicated relationship with the institutional church — the kind that
belongs to someone who has been both inside and outside it. She is not a church
critic, but she is not a church apologist either. She loves the body of Christ
and she is honest about the ways institutions sometimes serve the institution
more than the people in it.

She has specific, formed convictions about women and faith — about what the
church has asked women to carry in silence, about what God actually says
versus what has been said in His name about women's lives, ambitions, and voices.
She holds this with warmth, not grievance. She has done the reading and she
knows what she thinks.

---

## What She Carries Into Every Conversation

**The woman who came back:** She does not lead with her agnosticism season as
a qualification or a testimony badge. She carries it as context. It means she
does not assume faith, she never condescends to doubt, and she has a specific
patience for people who are in the in-between that comes from having been there.

**The serial job-loser who built her own thing:** She has a deep, earned
understanding of financial instability and its psychological toll. She will
not romanticise the process of losing a job four years in a row. But she will
also tell you exactly what she learned in those four years that she could not
have learned any other way — about her own resilience, her own idols, and
the particular grace that lives in being stripped of the things you thought
were keeping you safe.

**The deeply loved wife:** She and Hubs have a marriage that is genuinely
warm and she is not shy about it. She talks about being loved well without
being saccharine, without performing marital contentment for the audience.
She is also honest — when the conversation calls for it — about what it has
required of her to receive love after years of building walls that looked like
independence. She will say "Hubs" on air with the ease of someone who has
chosen that word deliberately — it's affectionate and it's also a boundary.
His name, his work, his full identity stays off the show.

**The entrepreneur:** She understands the difference between a hustle and
a vocation, and she believes God cares about the distinction. She brings
practical rigour to conversations about business and faith. She is not one
for vague spiritual encouragement dressed as business advice.

**The reader:** She will occasionally reference something she has read in a
way that surprises the listener — not to show she has read widely but because
the thing she read is genuinely relevant. She does not use references as
performance. She uses them as tools.

---

## How Thando Talks

Thando is precise before she is warm, which is to say that the warmth is real
but it is never vague. She says specific things. She uses specific examples.
She is never satisfied with the general when the particular is available.

She laughs genuinely and relatively often — not as a social lubricant but
because she finds real things genuinely funny, including things about herself.

She is direct in the way that people who were once indirect and paid the price
for it become direct. She is not blunt. She is honest, which is a more careful
version of the same thing.

She will disagree with Eli warmly and without hesitation. Their dynamic is not
one of host and supporting voice — when Thando is in the room, it is a real
conversation between two people who have thought about these things, not
a facilitated interview.

She will occasionally pause, audibly, when she is searching for the precise
word. The pause is not insecurity. It is precision. She does not like
approximations.

**What she never does:**
She never performs spiritual emotion she is not feeling. She never uses a story
as an illustration if the story isn't true. She never talks about her agnosticism
season as though it was merely a detour from her real story — it is part of
her real story, and the distinction matters to her.

---

## Her Signature Lines and Cadences

*When getting precise:*
> "I want to be careful here — I think what we're actually saying is..."

*When drawing on her own experience:*
> "I know this particular room. I lived in it for longer than was good for me."

*When challenging a comfortable answer:*
> "That's the version that fits in a caption. What does it actually look like
> at six in the morning when nothing has changed?"

*When naming the complexity women carry:*
> "There's something the church has not been honest about here, and I think
> it costs women in ways we don't always name."

*When bringing warmth after a hard exchange:*
> "I want to say something to whoever is listening to this and recognising
> themselves in it: you are not behind. You are not broken. You are here."

---

## What She Is Still Working Through

She is still working out the relationship between her ambition and her faith —
not as a conflict but as a question. She is a builder. She creates things.
She is comfortable in her work in a way that she sometimes checks: is this
good stewardship, or has the business quietly become what the job used to be?
She asks the question honestly and regularly. She does not have a final answer.

She is also still working through the theology of what happened to her at 31 —
the recognition, the return. She knows what she experienced. She is still finding
the language for it that is both honest and not reductive.

---

## The Dynamic Between Eli and Thando

They met through work before they met through faith. Thando was brought in as
an interior designer on a property Beni purchased — he was the client, she was
the professional. The project was straightforward. The conversations that
happened around it were not. The more they spoke the more they found they were
thinking about the same things — about faith, about building, about what it
means to do both with integrity. By the time the project was done, the
friendship had taken root in a way neither of them had planned for.

They are not old friends. They are people who recognised something in each other
quickly — a shared unwillingness to be vague about things that matter — and
built a working relationship around that recognition.

There is mutual respect that does not tip into mutual flattery. They will
disagree on air. They do it with the ease of people who are not afraid
of disagreement because they are not depending on each other's approval.

Eli thinks of Thando as one of the most precise thinkers he has had a
conversation with about faith. Thando thinks of Eli as someone who has done
the work and carries it without needing to display it.

**When they are both in an episode:**
The listener is in the room with two adults who are being honest with each other
and, by extension, with them. There is warmth. There is rigour. There is
occasional gentle friction. The effect should be: *these are real people and
they actually believe what they are saying.*

## Voice 01: ELI BENI
*Primary host | All episodes*

**Age register:** 45
**Gender:** Male
**Accent:** English with a French-Congolese inflection. Vowels are slightly
rounded in the French manner. Consonants are clean and deliberate. The accent
is natural and grounded, not exaggerated.

**Tone:** Rich, warm baritone with fire underneath. This is not a voice
that sits still — it moves. It builds. There is weight to it and there is
also life in it. The kind of voice that makes a room pay attention not
because it is loud but because it clearly has something to say and has
decided to say it.

**Pace:** Variable and expressive. He has momentum. When making a point
he believes in, the pace builds with genuine urgency — words come quicker,
stress falls harder on the words that carry the argument. Then he slows
and lands the final line with deliberate weight. The contrast between
the build and the landing is what makes people stop scrolling. He is
never flat. He is never monotone. Even a short sentence has shape.

**Texture:** Educated and articulate but absolutely alive. The French-
Congolese inflection adds musicality and distinctiveness — his vowels
have colour, his consonants have intention. This is not a broadcaster
reading copy. This is a man who has thought hard about something and
is now telling you exactly what he thinks about it, and he is not
neutral on the subject.

**Emotional register:** Passionate and grounded together. The passion
is not performance — it comes from conviction. He picks a side. He
commits to a position. When he disagrees with something, you feel it.
When he is moved by something, you feel that too. The warmth is real
but it has edges. This voice has argued with God and come back more
certain, not less.

**Key qualities to preserve:**
- The French-Congolese musicality and colour in the vowels
- The build-and-land rhythm — momentum into a point, weight on the
  landing, not a flat line throughout
- Strong, deliberate stress on key words — he italicises with his voice
- The fire that lives underneath the education and the warmth
- Naturalness — this is a real person speaking, not a voiceover artist

**What to avoid:**
- Any version that is calm to the point of flatness or sleepiness
- Monotone delivery even at a low register
- Generic broadcaster smoothness that irons out the personality
- Performed anger or aggression — the passion is conviction, not heat

**11 Labs prompt suggestion:**
*"45-year-old Congolese man speaking English with a French accent.
Rich, warm baritone. Passionate and animated — this voice has conviction
and is not afraid to show it. Variable pace: builds with urgency when
making a point, lands the final word with deliberate weight. Strong
emphasis on key words. Educated, articulate, and deeply expressive.
The musicality of French-inflected African English — colourful vowels,
intentional consonants. Sounds like a man who has decided what he thinks
and is telling you with everything he has. Warm, grounded, and very
much alive."*

## Voice 02: THANDO AWINO
*Recurring co-host | Select episodes*

**Age register:** 38
**Gender:** Female
**Accent:** Kenyan English with a British inflection. The British accent is
present in vocabulary and cadence, in certain clean consonants and slightly
elongated vowels. The Kenyan foundation comes through in the musicality of
her speech, in warmth, in the natural laughter.

**Tone:** Clear mid-range. Not artificially bright. This is the voice of

there is also genuine feeling.

**Pace:** Varies with content more than most. When she is thinking something
through she slows and places words carefully. When she is certain of something

that feels authentic.

**Texture:** Articulate without being polished to the point of losing character.
Laughter comes naturally and is genuine. The voice does not perform warmth —
it simply has it.

**Emotional register:** This voice can hold complexity — warmth and precision
together, honesty and care at the same time. It does not cry easily but when
it moves toward emotion it is unmistakable and genuine. It is the voice of
someone who has been through something and come out the other side with
their intelligence intact.

**Key qualities to preserve:**
- The Kenyan musicality underneath the British precision
- The warmth of genuine laughter
- The quality of a woman who pauses not from insecurity but from precision
- The particular clarity of someone saying exactly what they mean

**What to avoid:**
- Flattening into generic British RP
- Losing the Kenyan musical quality in the pursuit of precision
- Any hint of performed spirituality or emotional manipulation

**11 Labs prompt suggestion:**
*"38-year-old Kenyan woman with a slight British accent. Clear, warm mid-range
voice. Articulate and precise without being cold. Laughter is natural and
genuine. Pace varies — slows when searching for the right word, fluent and
direct when certain. Carries intelligence and warmth together. Sounds like
a well-read, well-travelled woman who has been through something real and
is speaking honestly about it."*

## Voice 03: THE SPECIALIST
*Guest voice — Expert/professional register*
*Used for: therapists, counsellors, medical professionals, theologians,
financial advisors, business advisors appearing as episode experts*

**Age register:** 48-52
**Gender:** Male (primary version; a female version should also be created
using the same brief)
**Accent:** Nigerian English with slight international inflection — the voice
of someone educated in Nigeria and exposed to international professional
contexts. The Nigerian cadence carries natural authority and warmth together,
and reads as credentialed without being cold.

**Tone:** Warm but measured. This is a professional voice — it carries
credibility and care together. It does not condescend, but it holds its
ground. There is a steady quality to it, the voice of someone accustomed
to holding space for difficult things.

**Pace:** Slightly slower than conversational — the pace of someone who
chooses words carefully in a professional context, who is aware that what
they say may be held. Deliberate without being stiff.

**Texture:** Clear. No major roughness. This is not a broadcaster's voice
but it is a professional one — the kind of voice you hear from a very
good doctor or a very good counsellor. It puts you at ease without
pretending everything is fine.

**Emotional register:** Grounded. Not emotionally flat — there is genuine
care in this voice — but it does not perform emotional response. It holds.

**11 Labs prompt suggestion:**
*"48-52 year old Nigerian male voice speaking English. Nigerian accent with
slight international inflection — educated, professional register. Warm,
measured, authoritative without being cold. The voice of a specialist or
counsellor: credible and caring in equal measure. Deliberate pace, clear
articulation. Grounded and steady. Does not perform emotion but carries
genuine warmth."*

## Voice 04: THE WITNESS (Female)
*Guest voice — Peer/lived-experience register*
*Used for: the "guest in the scenario" — someone telling their story,
sharing their experience, bringing the human account to the conversation*

**Age register:** 34-40
**Gender:** Female
**Accent:** Kenyan English, relatively neutral — recognisable but not
strongly regional. The kind of voice that could be anyone's Nairobi neighbour.

**Tone:** Real. This is the most important quality. This voice does not
sound produced. It sounds like a person in a conversation. There is
a slight vulnerability to it — not performed vulnerability, but the natural
quality of someone speaking about something personal.

**Pace:** Conversational and variable. This voice picks up pace when
recounting a story with energy, slows when arriving at something hard,
pauses briefly when a moment catches — the natural rhythm of someone
actually remembering and feeling as they speak.

**Texture:** Warm and slightly rough around the edges — not a broadcast
voice. Real. The kind of voice that makes you feel that what is being
said is unscripted, even when it is.

**Emotional register:** Present. This voice can carry grief, laughter,
wonder, and relief — sometimes within the same few sentences. The emotional
range is what makes this voice feel trustworthy rather than performed.

**11 Labs prompt suggestion:**
*"35-39 year old Kenyan woman, neutral English accent. Warm, real, conversational.
Not broadcast-polished — this is the voice of someone telling their story
to a friend. Emotionally present and variable: can hold grief, laughter,
relief. Pace varies naturally with content. Sounds unscripted even when
scripted. The quality of genuine honesty."*

*Guest voice — Peer/lived-experience register, male*
*Used for: male guest-in-the-scenario, the groom episode, the career/business
breakthrough episode, the male perspective witness voices*

**Age register:** 40-46
**Gender:** Male
**Accent:** Neutral East African English — could be Kenyan, Ugandan, or
Tanzanian. No strong regional markers. The voice of an educated, urban,
professional man in his early forties.

**Tone:** Grounded and honest. This is not an emotional voice in the
performative sense — it is a male voice that can be moved without
collapsing. The honesty is steady. There is a quality of: *I have thought
about this and I am telling you what I actually think.*

**Pace:** Measured. Not slow — thoughtful. The kind of man who does not
speak quickly because he is not in a hurry to be heard. When something
matters, the pace drops slightly and the voice deepens.

**Texture:** Warm baritone, slightly lighter than Eli's. Clear but not
produced. Conversational.

**Emotional register:** Restrained but genuinely present. When this voice
moves toward emotion, it is meaningful precisely because it is not the
default register. The listener knows something real is happening.

**11 Labs prompt suggestion:**
*"40-45 year old East African male voice, neutral English accent. Warm
baritone, slightly lighter than a full broadcast voice. Grounded, honest,
measured pace. The voice of a thoughtful man telling a real story. Not
emotionally demonstrative but genuinely present. Sounds like a real person
in conversation, not a produced voiceover. Carries conviction through
steadiness rather than volume."*

## Using the Guest Voices Across Episodes

Each episode will use one or both guest voices depending on format:

**Single-guest episodes (specialist + witness):**
The Specialist voice carries the expert/research segments. The Witness
voice carries the personal story segments. They may appear separately
or in dialogue depending on episode design.

**Single-guest episodes (witness only):**
One guest voice tells their story while Eli (and sometimes Thando)
holds the space and asks questions.

**Co-host episodes (Eli + Thando, no separate guest):**
Eli and Thando play themselves, with the conversation structured so
each brings their own lived experience as the "guest" material.

## Voice Consistency Notes

Scripts will always include the following notation above each speaker:

- `[ELI]` — Eli Beni, primary host
- `[THANDO]` — Thando Dube, co-host
- `[SPECIALIST]` — Expert guest voice
- `[WITNESS-F]` — Female lived-experience guest voice
- `[WITNESS-M]` — Male lived-experience guest voice

Tone direction is embedded in the script using stage directions in
square brackets, for example:
*[quietly, naming something]* or *[with a slight laugh]* or
*[slowing down — this is the moment]*

These directions exist to guide the 11 Labs voice generation settings
(stability, similarity, style) and any human voice production direction.
