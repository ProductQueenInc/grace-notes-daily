# BETWEEN SUNDAYS
## Episode 01: "God Didn't Promise You That Job"
### Full Episode Script — Version 2

**Host:** Thando Dube
**Guests:**
- Kofi — 41, Ghanaian, based in Nairobi. Retrenched at 38 with a pregnant wife at home. [KOFI]
- Sonia — 36, Zimbabwean, based in Johannesburg. Fired for refusing to falsify data. [SONIA]

**Target length:** 30 minutes
**Voice labels:** [THANDO] | [KOFI] | [SONIA]

## COLD OPEN
*No music. Thando straight in. Quiet and direct.*

[THANDO]

Nobody tells you about the morning after.

You lose the job — and that day is terrible, obviously. But it's actually the morning after that gets you. When you wake up and for about three seconds everything is normal. And then it lands. And you lie there trying to figure out what to do with yourself. What to do with your hands. Where to put all the energy that used to have somewhere to go.

I know that morning. I had it four years in a row.

And today I want to talk about it. Not to have a pity party — trust me, we are well past that. But because I think there are things that happen to a person in that season that we don't talk about honestly enough. The faith questions. The shame. The anger. And also — because I have seen it — the completely inexplicable ways that God shows up when everything else has gone quiet.

I have two people with me today who know what I'm talking about. And I think you need to hear them.

## GUEST INTRODUCTIONS

[THANDO] *— warm, unhurried*

Kofi has been a friend of mine for a while now. He's in finance, based here in Nairobi, and he went through something a few years back that I think a lot of men specifically are going to hear themselves in. Kofi, welcome.

[KOFI] *— warm, slightly self-deprecating*

Thank you. Happy to be here. Mostly.

[THANDO] *— laughing lightly*

"Mostly." I'll take it. And Sonia is joining us from Johannesburg. She and I met through a mutual friend and when she told me her story I said — this one needs to be in a room. Sonia, thank you for coming.

[SONIA] *— clear, composed*

Thank you for having me. I've been looking forward to this actually.

[THANDO]

Okay. So let's not do the polished version of these stories. I want the real one. Kofi — take me back to the day it happened.

---

## KOFI'S STORY

[KOFI] *— taking a breath, going back there*

Right. So I was thirty-eight. Senior finance manager, multinational company, had been there six years. My wife Abena was seven months pregnant with our first child. And I got called in on a Thursday afternoon and was told the regional office was restructuring and my role was being eliminated. Effective end of the month.

[THANDO]

How did you feel in the room?

[KOFI]

Honestly? I didn't feel anything in the room. I nodded. I shook hands. I said the right things. I think I even thanked them. *[small, embarrassed laugh]* I thanked them.

And then I walked to my car. And I sat there for — I don't know how long. And what came wasn't sadness, really. What came was this very specific thought: I cannot go home and tell Abena this.

[THANDO]

Why not?

[KOFI]

Because she was seven months pregnant. Because I was supposed to be the provider. Because in my head, in the version of myself I had built, that was the one thing I was there to do. And I had just... stopped being able to do it. And I couldn't face that. I couldn't face what I thought I would see on her face.

[THANDO] *— quietly*

So what did you do?

[KOFI]

I went home. I told her it had been a long day. And the next morning I got up at the same time, put on the same clothes, kissed her goodbye, and went to a café near our old office. And I sat there. Every day. For two weeks.

[SONIA] *— genuinely shocked, but without judgment*

Two weeks.

[KOFI]

Two weeks. Laptop open. Doing nothing. Ordering coffee I didn't want. Going home at the same time. Lying through my teeth.

[THANDO]

Were you praying during that time?

[KOFI] *— honest pause*

No. Not really. I mean, I said the words at dinner when we prayed together as a couple. But on my own? No. I think I was too ashamed to pray. Like — God could already see what I was doing, and I couldn't add words to it. I just went quiet.

[THANDO]

And something about that actually makes complete sense to me. Because there's a psalm — Psalm 46 — that says God is an ever-present help in trouble. And what gets me about that is the *ever-present* part. Not "available if you call." Present. Already there. Even in the café. Even in the lying. He was in that too.

[KOFI] *— nodding slowly*

Yeah. I couldn't see it then. But yeah.

[THANDO]

What broke it open?

[KOFI]

Abena. One night she asked me to pray over the baby. We used to do that — she'd lie down and I'd put my hand on her stomach and pray. And I opened my mouth and nothing came out. I just — I started crying. Right there. Couldn't stop.

And she looked at me for a long moment and she said: "How long?"

And I told her everything.

[SONIA] *— softly*

What did she do?

[KOFI]

She didn't panic. She didn't cry. She just took my hand and she said: "Then we trust God together." That was it. "Then we trust God together."

*[brief silence]*

And I think that was the moment something in me actually broke open properly. Because I had been carrying this alone for two weeks and — I mean, she already knew. Of course she already knew. She just waited for me to be ready to say it.

[THANDO]

She was holding the space for you.

[KOFI]

She was. Yeah.

[THANDO]

And then what happened practically? Because that's the thing people want to know — okay, you told her, beautiful moment, but the bills don't stop.

[KOFI] *— slight laugh*

Right. The bills absolutely did not stop. So — look. What happened next I genuinely cannot fully explain. A guy I had worked with three years earlier, hadn't spoken to in a long time, called me out of nowhere and asked if I could consult on a project. Paid well. Then my uncle — and my uncle and I are not close, we don't have that kind of relationship — called Abena directly and said he wanted to help with the rent for three months. She hadn't told him anything. He just called.

[SONIA]

That's — how does that happen?

[KOFI]

That's what I mean. I don't know how it happens. But there's this story in 1 Kings — Elijah is hiding, there's a drought, no food anywhere, and God sends ravens to bring him bread and meat every morning and evening. Ravens. Which — if you know anything about Jewish tradition — are unclean birds. The last source you'd expect. And I kept thinking about that. God was feeding Elijah through ravens. The provision came. It just didn't come from where anyone expected it to.

[THANDO]

And Jesus picks that story up too. He says — consider the ravens, they don't plant, they don't harvest, they have no storehouse, and yet God feeds them. He was making the same point centuries later. God's provision tends to come through channels that don't make conventional sense.

[KOFI]

And I had to let go of what I thought provision was supposed to look like. That was the real work.

---

## SONIA'S STORY

[THANDO]

Sonia, your situation was — different. Because you didn't lose your job through a restructure. You lost it for a different reason entirely.

[SONIA] *— clear, no bitterness but no softening either*

I was fired. For doing the right thing.

[THANDO]

Tell us.

[SONIA]

I was in audit. My manager asked me to sign off on a client report that had numbers in it I knew were wrong. Not mistakes — intentionally wrong. And I said no. I documented my concerns, I escalated, I gave them every opportunity to correct it. And three days after I refused to sign, I was called into HR and told I was being let go due to "performance concerns."

[KOFI] *— quiet disbelief*

After how long at the company?

[SONIA]

Four years. Good reviews. No prior issues. Gone in three days.

[THANDO]

How did you feel?

[SONIA]

Furious. *[small laugh]* I was absolutely furious. Not just at the company — at God. Because here's the thing. Two months before this happened, I had been offered another job. Good role, different company. And I turned it down because I felt — I genuinely felt — God saying stay. I had prayed about it, I had talked to Hubs about it, I had peace about staying. And then I stayed. And I did the right thing. And I got fired for it.

So my question to God was not gentle. It was: what exactly was the point of that?

[THANDO] *— nodding, not rushing past this*

That question deserves to sit for a second. Because I think a lot of Christians pretend they haven't asked it. Or they rush to the answer before they've actually sat with the question.

[SONIA]

And I wasn't going to do that. I was not going to perform peace I didn't have. There's a passage in Habakkuk — and Habakkuk is a book nobody reads, which is a shame — where he is essentially saying to God: I keep calling for help and you're not answering. Why are you making me watch injustice happen and doing nothing? And what I found remarkable about that book is that God doesn't rebuke him. He doesn't say "how dare you speak to me that way." He *answers*. He engages the question. And that gave me permission to keep showing up to God even when I was angry. Because at least I was showing up.

[KOFI]

That's an important distinction. Angry at God but still talking to God.

[SONIA]

Yes. I wasn't walking away. I was in His face about it. Which is — different.

[THANDO]

It's actually closer, in a strange way.

[SONIA]

It's much closer. And I think about Job a lot in this context. Because God himself says — at the beginning of the book — that Job is blameless. There is no one like him. And then allows everything to be taken from him. And Job's friends spend the whole book trying to convince him there must be something he did wrong, because their theology cannot accommodate innocent suffering. And at the end of the book God looks at those friends — the ones with the tidy answers — and says: you did not speak what is right, as my servant Job did. Job, who argued, who demanded, who was furious. He spoke what was right.

[KOFI]

So the honest anger is more acceptable to God than the polished theology.

[SONIA]

That's exactly what that text is saying. Yes.

[THANDO] *— letting that land*

Yeah. That's not a small thing.

[SONIA]

It kept me. Honestly. That and one sentence an older woman at my church said to me about three months in. I was a mess. And she said: "You kept your integrity. That is not nothing. That is actually everything." And I didn't fully believe her then. But I wrote it down.

---

## THREE-WAY CONVERSATION

[THANDO]

I want to ask both of you something. Because between the two of you, you've covered something I think is really important — the difference between losing something through circumstances, like Kofi, and losing something through *choosing right*, like Sonia. But there's a third version, which is what I lived. Four years of just — consecutive losses. No dramatic reason. Just one after another after another.

And what I found in year three was that I started questioning whether any of it meant something. Like — is there a plan? Or is this just random? And I had to land somewhere with that question.

[KOFI]

Where did you land?

[THANDO]

I landed on Lamentations 3. Which is one of the most honest books in the Bible about sitting in wreckage. The writer is in genuine ruin. And in the middle of it he says — "His mercies are new every morning." And what got me was that he says that in the same breath as admitting he is in pain. It's not a pivot away from the pain. It's a mercy that lives inside it. And I held onto that. Not always with confidence. Sometimes just with — stubbornness.

[SONIA] *— with warmth*

Stubborn faith is still faith.

[THANDO]

It really is. *[light laugh]*

Kofi, I want to ask you something specific. Because you said the real work was letting go of what you thought provision was supposed to look like. What does that actually mean practically? Because I think that's the thing people struggle with most — the gap between what they're praying for and what's actually arriving.

[KOFI]

So — there's this thing in Exodus that I think about. The manna. The Israelites are in the desert, they're hungry, God sends manna every morning. But there are two rules. One: you can only collect enough for one day. Whoever collected more found it rotted by morning. And two: you can't gather it on the Sabbath — God provides double the day before. So the whole system is designed to make you show up again tomorrow. You cannot stockpile. You cannot get ahead of it.

[THANDO]

It's daily, by design.

[KOFI]

By design. And I had to understand that my provision during that season was manna. It came daily. It was enough. It was not what I had planned for and it was not what I would have chosen. But it was there every morning. And the spiritual work was trusting that tomorrow's would also come, without me trying to force it.

[SONIA]

That's so different from how we think about financial security, though. We are taught to plan, to save, to project ahead. And manna is the opposite of all of that.

[KOFI]

Completely the opposite. And I'm not saying don't plan — I'm still a finance manager, planning is my job. But there's a difference between responsible stewardship and anxious control. And what job loss does, if you let it, is teach you the difference.

[THANDO]

Sonia, what about you? The restoration took two years, you said. That's a long time to hold something. What kept you?

[SONIA]

Honestly? A verse in Galatians. Chapter 6. *"Do not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time you will reap a harvest if you do not give up."* And the phrase I kept coming back to was "at the proper time." Not my time. Not the time I needed it. The proper time. Which is — that's either the most comforting thing in the Bible or the most frustrating, depending on the day.

[KOFI] *— laughing*

Depending entirely on the day.

[SONIA]

But what it does is take the timeline out of my hands. And there was something freeing in that, eventually. Because I had been trying to manage the timeline. Trying to make things happen faster, sending more applications, having more conversations, working harder to compensate for what had been taken. And nothing was moving. And at some point I had to release it. I had to say — okay. Not my timeline. Yours.

[THANDO]

And then?

[SONIA]

And then things started moving. Not because I stopped working — I didn't stop working. But I stopped straining. And there's a difference between working from faith and working from fear. When I stopped working from fear, something shifted.

[THANDO] *— sitting with this*

Working from faith versus working from fear. I think that's the thing, isn't it. Because the job search itself — the applications, the interviews, the rejections — all of that is real and it has to be done. But the energy you bring to it changes what you find. Or maybe what finds you.

[KOFI]

And who you are when you walk into the room. People can feel desperation. It's not attractive in an interview. But they can also feel — I don't know what the word is—

[SONIA]

Groundedness?

[KOFI]

Groundedness. Yes. When you come in knowing that this particular job is not the whole of your worth or the whole of your future — that comes across too.

[THANDO]

What would you both say to the person listening right now who is in the middle of it? Not out of it yet. Actually in it.

[KOFI] *— direct, warm*

Tell someone. That's the first thing. Not everyone — you don't have to announce it to the world. But one person. The hiding makes it heavier. I tried to carry it alone for two weeks and it nearly broke me. The moment I told Abena, even though I was terrified, was the moment God had somewhere to work.

[SONIA]

I would say: your anger is allowed. Your questions are allowed. God is not fragile. He has handled much larger arguments than yours, and He has never walked away from someone for being honest about what they're feeling. Habakkuk was furious. Job was furious. David in Psalm 13 says — "How long, Lord? Will you forget me forever?" That is in the Bible. God left it there. He's fine with the question. Be honest with Him.

[THANDO]

And I would add — be careful what you use to fill the silence. Because when things go quiet, we reach for something to manage the anxiety. Some people reach for distraction, some for overwork, some for things that aren't good for them. And I understand it, I genuinely do. But the silence is also where God does some of His most important work. It's not comfortable. But it is not wasted.

---

## GRACE NOTE CLOSE

[THANDO] *— quieter now, slower*

I want to close with something.

There's a story in 1 Kings 17 that I keep coming back to. Elijah, the prophet. He's just done something extraordinary — he's called down fire from heaven, he's seen God move in an undeniable way. And then immediately after, he runs. He's exhausted, he's afraid, and he sits under a tree in the desert and says to God: I'm done. Take my life. I have nothing left.

And what does God do?

He doesn't rebuke him. He doesn't remind him of what just happened, or tell him to pull himself together. An angel shows up. Touches him. And says: get up and eat. There's bread. There's water. Get up and eat.

And he does. And then he lies back down. And the angel comes again. And says: get up and eat. The journey is too much for you.

*The journey is too much for you.*

Not — you are too weak. Not — you should have more faith. Just — this is too much. And here is food. And here is rest. And I know that.

Whatever you are carrying today — God knows it is too much for one person. He is not waiting for you to manage it better. He is bringing bread. He is telling you to eat. He is telling you the journey is too much and that is not a failure — that is just honest.

You don't have to be okay today. You just have to eat the bread that's in front of you and trust that there will be more in the morning.

I'm Thando. This is Between Sundays. We'll see you next week.

*Music comes in softly as her last line lands.*
